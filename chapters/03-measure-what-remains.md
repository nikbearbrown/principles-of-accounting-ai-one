# Measure what remains

A technician takes seven identical parts from a drawer. The physical count is easy: three remain. The financial question takes longer. Six units arrived at $20 each and four at $25 each. Which dollars have been used?

By the end, you can reconcile units and costs, distinguish a cost-flow assumption from actual movement, and explain why depreciation changes income without moving cash this month.

## Keep the unit roll-forward separate

Opening units: zero. Received: ten. Issued on finished work: seven. Counted at close: three. Investigate a mismatch before assigning a value. Seven issued units plus three remaining units accounts for all ten; it says nothing yet about the $220 spent or owed. The invoice establishes cost; the count and issue record establish quantity.

Under **first in, first out (FIFO)** for this example, the seven units absorb the oldest six costs ($120) and one unit from the $25 lot. Debit parts expense $145; credit inventory $145. The remaining three carry $75. The book costs add back to $220.

Under a **single periodic weighted-average cost** for the same receipts, cost per unit is $220 / 10 = $22. Issuing seven units allocates $154 to expense and leaves $66. Both reconcile, but the expense differs by $9. This is not evidence that the technician picked different physical parts or that bank cash is $9 lower. It is the consequence of a different formula for allocating the same total cost across periods. Do not mix the FIFO expense with the average-cost ending balance.

The [IFRS Foundation's IAS 2 summary](https://www.ifrs.org/issued-standards/list-of-standards/ias-2-inventories/) discusses interchangeable items, cost formulas and measurement at the lower of cost and net realizable value. This classroom case stipulates no write-down, inventory loss or returns. It does not select a reporting framework for a real shop.

## An asset has a schedule, not just a price tag

An existing machine cost $600. The exercise assumes a 20-month useful life, zero residual value and straight-line depreciation. The method allocates $30 a month: ($600 − $0) / 20. With $120 accumulated depreciation at the start of May, the carrying amount is $480. At the end of May, before new equipment, accumulated depreciation is $150 and the carrying amount is $450. Record a $30 expense and credit accumulated depreciation, a separate account that reduces reported equipment.

Market value is not calculated here. An observable sale price for a used machine need not equal a balance produced by a systematic cost allocation. Depreciation also does not move cash in May; the original purchase happened before the month. Suppose new equipment costs $240 in cash on May 31 but will be available for use June 1. This case records the equipment purchase in May and starts its depreciation in June. The cash outflow and the first depreciation charge happen on different schedules.

The [IFRS Foundation's IAS 16 summary](https://www.ifrs.org/issued-standards/list-of-standards/ias-16-property-plant-and-equipment/) gives background on equipment recognition and measurement. A real useful-life, impairment or expense classification decision needs its own facts.

## Practice and check

**Inventory.** If only five parts are issued, use FIFO to compute expense and ending inventory. Then confirm the unit count and total cost.

**Equipment.** With the original May purchase and schedule, calculate closing gross equipment, accumulated depreciation, and net equipment.

**Checks.** Under FIFO, $100 goes to expense and $120 remains (one $20 unit and four $25 units); five units remain. Equipment is $840 gross less $150 accumulated depreciation, or $690 net. If you claimed $840 of cash movement in May, trace which asset was already on the opening balance sheet.
