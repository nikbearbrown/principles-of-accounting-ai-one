# Recognize the event, not just the payment

Three documents show exactly $120. One is an advance payment for next month's shop coverage. One is a bill for repair work already accepted by a customer. One is a deposit for a job still on the bench. The dollar sign matches. The evidence does not.

By the end of this chapter, you can distinguish prepaid expenses, receivables and customer advances, and post a period-end adjustment without repeating a cash entry.

## The same amount, three different clocks

Use simplified accrual accounting for a fictional repair shop. The receipt or payment date controls the cash ledger. For this exercise, revenue from a repair is recorded when the promised work is accepted. Expenses are charged when the related resources are consumed or the obligation has been incurred. Real arrangements can need more evidence and different treatment; the acceptance rule is specified here, not a universal contract interpretation.

| Evidence | Initial entry | What happens next? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pay $120 for May and June coverage, one half each | Debit prepaid coverage; credit cash $120 | As May coverage is consumed, debit coverage expense and credit prepaid coverage $60. |
| Finish an accepted $120 repair and invoice the customer | Debit receivable; credit service revenue $120 | When paid, debit cash and credit receivable. Do not recognize the revenue again. |
| Receive $120 for a repair accepted next month | Debit cash; credit customer advances $120 | When accepted under this exercise's rule, debit customer advances and credit service revenue. |

A **prepayment** is the business's right to something it has not consumed. A **customer advance** is the mirror position: the customer paid, but the business still owes performance under the case terms. An **account receivable** is a claim to cash after work has been accepted and billed. All three balances matter because something remains unresolved at the reporting date.

## Close at a date, not at a feeling

Suppose the coverage was paid May 1. At May 31 the shop has used one month of two. Adjust the prepaid account from $120 to $60 and record a $60 expense. The original cash withdrawal stays $120. An income statement charging the full withdrawal to May makes May's expenses too high and misses the unconsumed June coverage. An income statement with no charge makes May look better than its operating record supports.

Now suppose the shop owes $20 for May labor and will pay in June. Debit wage expense and credit wages payable $20 on May 31. Paying in June will decrease cash and the payable. This resembles an invoice received after the books close: the payment record is not the only place to look for costs already incurred.

The right way to work a timing problem is to write two sentences first. What has changed by the reporting date? What claim or obligation remains? Then choose accounts. A well-formed debit and credit cannot answer those questions for you.

## Read the negative case

A customer pays on May 27 and the shop prints an invoice on May 30. The job is accepted June 2. Assume the $120 is an advance, there are no partial performance rights, and the printed invoice is only a pro forma notice. On May 31 the customer advance is still a liability under the exercise's rule. Filing a May invoice does not create May earnings when its supporting event is in June.

## Practice and check

1. The shop prepays $180 for three equal months, including May. What is the May expense and remaining asset?
2. The $120 accepted repair has only $45 collected by May 31. What is the remaining receivable? How much May revenue came from that repair?
3. A student posts the $20 wage accrual by crediting cash. What can catch the error even though the entry balances?

**Checks.** The remaining prepaid asset is $120 after a $60 expense. The incomplete collection leaves $75 receivable and does not alter the $120 earned. Compare the supposed cash payment to the bank record and the time record; credit wages payable for the unpaid May amount. Track these timing choices into [the financial statements](04-read-the-statements-together.md).
